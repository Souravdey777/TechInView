**Short answer:** engineering manager interview questions fall into five families: people, delivery, technical judgment, stakeholders, and scope or org design. Prepare two real stories for each. Answer with situation, the decision you owned, the mechanism you put in place, the outcome, and what you would change.

These questions sound conversational and are scored hard. The panel is trying to work out one thing: when you say "we," what did *you* do? Strong senior engineers fail these loops by describing team outcomes without ever naming a decision they personally owned.

## The five families

| Family | Typical question | What the panel is scoring |
|---|---|---|
| People | "Tell me about someone you managed out." | Whether you act, and whether you act humanely |
| Delivery | "A quarter is slipping in week six. What do you do?" | Whether you replan or hope |
| Technical judgment | "When did you overrule an engineer?" | Whether you are still technically credible |
| Stakeholders | "Product wants a date you don't believe." | Whether you negotiate with evidence |
| Scope and org | "How did you split a team of twelve?" | Whether you think in systems, not headcount |

Most loops draw from all five. With two stories per family you have the round covered; with one, you will end up repeating yourself.

## The answer structure that works

STAR works for [behavioral rounds](/blog/behavioral-interview-software-engineer-star), but management answers need an extra beat: the **mechanism**. Panels want the process or system you put in place, not just the happy ending. Here is an illustrative example:

> - **Situation.** Two teams shipping to the same service, each blocked on the other's migrations.
> - **Decision.** I took ownership of the sequencing instead of letting them negotiate it every week.
> - **Mechanism.** One shared migration calendar, one owner per window, and a 15-minute Thursday sync that I ran for six weeks and then handed to a tech lead.
> - **Outcome.** Each migration went from taking about five weeks to nine days, and we stopped reverting.
> - **What I'd change.** I set up the calendar three weeks too late because I waited for consensus instead of deciding.

That last beat carries a lot of weight. A candidate who cannot name a mistake reads as either inexperienced or not reflective.

## Numbers you should be able to quote cold

Not vanity metrics. The ones that prove you were actually running something:

- Team size and shape (levels, senior/junior mix)
- How many people you hired and how many left, over what period
- One delivery metric before and after something you changed
- One reliability or quality number you were accountable for
- The last performance conversation you had that changed someone's trajectory

Vagueness here is the loudest sign of a tech lead describing themselves as a manager. Both roles are respectable. Say which one you held.

## Red flags panels listen for

- **"We" with no "I."** If no sentence contains a decision you made, you described a team, not a manager.
- **Only success stories.** Nobody who has had real reports has a clean record.
- **Process for its own sake.** Adding meetings or rituals without naming the problem they solved.
- **Blaming the org.** One mention of a constraint is context. Four is a pattern.
- **No technical opinion.** Panels check whether you can still spot a bad design.

## How to prepare in two weeks

1. **Inventory the last eighteen months.** Write twelve one-line situations and tag each with a family from the table. The gaps show you what to dig for.
2. **Write the mechanism beat for each.** Most people have the situation and outcome and skip the middle, which is the part being scored.
3. **Rehearse out loud, capped at three minutes.** Written answers run long and fall apart the moment someone interrupts.
4. **Prepare your own questions.** How they answer "what happens here when a team misses a quarter?" tells you more than the job description. See [turning a job description into an interview plan](/blog/job-description-interview-prep).

## FAQ

### I'm a tech lead moving into management. What do I say?

Say so directly, then lean on the closest real evidence: mentoring, owning on-call, driving a cross-team project, giving hard feedback. Do not inflate a lead role into a management one. Panels probe, and it falls apart.

### Are there coding rounds in EM loops?

Often, yes, and they are usually lighter than an IC loop's, but do not count on skipping them. Keep your fundamentals warm with something like the [90-day prep plan](/blog/90-day-coding-interview-prep-plan), scaled down.

### How much detail about people problems is appropriate?

Enough to show your reasoning, anonymized, and never dismissive of the person. How you talk about a former report is itself evidence.
