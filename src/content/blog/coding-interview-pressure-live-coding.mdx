**Short answer:** practice in the same conditions you will be tested in. That means a timer, talking out loud the whole time, and someone (or something) interrupting you mid-solution. Four weeks of drills that add those conditions one at a time will do more for live performance than another hundred silent submissions.

Knowing an algorithm is different from coding it while someone watches. Nerves eat into working memory, and silence leaves the interviewer guessing whether you are stuck or thinking. This post pairs well with [what FAANG interviewers score](/blog/faang-interview-scoring) and [how to think out loud](/blog/coding-interview-communication).

## Why silent LeetCode practice mis-trains you

| Silent practice | Live interview |
|-----------------|----------------|
| Pause as long as you like | Someone is waiting while you think |
| No need to explain anything | Narration is expected |
| Typing is the only task | Talking and typing compete for attention |
| Failed test, rerun, move on | You explain the failure calmly, out loud |

The skill you need is speaking and coding at the same time, and you only get it by practicing both together.

## The pressure ladder (four weeks)

### Week 1: read out loud, no code

Do ten problems where you spend the first five minutes only reading and reasoning out loud, using the steps in [how to read a coding interview problem](/blog/how-to-read-coding-interview-problems). Record one session and count your filler words.

### Week 2: the 25 + 15 split

Give yourself 25 minutes to implement and 15 minutes to refactor and test, talking the whole time. When the timer ends, stop, even mid-line. Then write a short post-mortem: what broke once the clock was running?

### Week 3: simulate interruptions

On every problem, interrupt yourself twice at random moments with a question like "what about empty input?" Stop, answer, and get back to where you were. Recovering cleanly from an interruption is a big part of what interviewers notice.

### Week 4: full dress rehearsal

Two 45-minute sessions this week, with a voice mock interviewer or a peer who follows a script. [AI vs human mock interviews](/blog/ai-mock-interview-2026) covers how to choose.

## Techniques for the round itself

- **Label your silence:** "I'm deciding between a hash map and sorting, one second."
- **State invariants before nested loops:** "At the top of this loop, everything left of `i` is already processed."
- **Debug on the smallest input:** say your hypothesis before changing any code.
- **Finish with complexity:** take a breath, then give Big-O and justify it from the loops. The [time complexity guide](/blog/time-complexity-cheat-sheet) is good drilling material.

## Manage your body, not just the algorithm

- The night before a real loop, sleep matters more than one more problem.
- A few slow breaths before you start, for example box breathing (in for 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4), helps many people settle.
- Warm up 30 minutes before with one easy problem and a spoken recap. Do not attempt a hard problem that could shake your confidence.

## FAQ

### I blank on easy problems when I'm nervous

Anxiety is using up the working memory you need. Drill easy problems on short timers while speaking until they are boring. Boring is the goal. [Valid Parentheses](/practice/valid-parentheses) and [Two Sum](/practice/two-sum) are good candidates.

### Should I memorize code templates?

Memorize patterns, not exact code. Interviewers change the problem slightly to see whether you can adapt. The [Two Sum walkthrough](/blog/two-sum-interview) shows how one pattern stretches across variants.

### What's the fastest way to simulate real pressure?

A voice mock with code that actually runs.
