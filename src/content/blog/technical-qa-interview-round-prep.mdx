**Short answer:** a **technical Q&A interview** checks whether you understand how the tools on your resume actually work. Strong answers explain the mechanism (what happens underneath), give evidence from something you built or debugged, and name where the approach stops working. Trivia recall is not the bar.

Candidates tend to prepare least for this round. There is no editor, no test suite and no algorithm to lean on, just questions about the language and frameworks you said you know.

## What this round is

Usually around 45 minutes of conversation pinned to your primary stack, starting with calibration questions and moving toward depth. It exists because someone can pass an algorithms round and still not understand why their service falls over at 3am. Unlike a [DSA round](/blog/faang-interview-scoring), there is no compiler to check your answers, so the interviewer's main signal is how precise your explanations are.

## The five question families

| Family | Sounds like | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | "What actually happens when this runs?" | Whether you know the layer below the API you use |
| Debugging scenario | "This works locally and fails in production. Go." | Forming and testing hypotheses |
| Trade-off | "Why this and not that?" | Judgement rather than preference |
| Failure mode | "How does this break under load?" | Operational experience |
| Verification | "How would you know it worked?" | Testing and observability habits |

If you can answer all five for the three main tools on your resume, you are in good shape for most stack-depth rounds. If you can only answer the first family, you will sound like the documentation.

## The shape of a strong answer

Four parts, in order: **claim, mechanism, evidence, limits.**

> "Yes, that endpoint will be slow. The ORM issues one query per row because the relation is lazy-loaded, so a page of fifty items turns into fifty-one round trips. I have seen this show up as a steady latency floor that disappears once you eager-load. The catch is that eager loading over-fetches on endpoints that only need the ids, so I would turn it on per query rather than globally."

Weak answers stop after the claim. Notice how little of that example is jargon and how much is cause and effect.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "React re-renders because state changed" | "The parent re-rendered and passed a new object as a prop, so `React.memo`'s shallow comparison failed and the child re-rendered too" |
| "We used Kafka for scale" | "We needed to replay events after consumer bugs, which our previous queue could not do" |
| "Indexes make queries faster" | "That query filters on `tenant_id` and sorts by `created_at`, so a composite index on `(tenant_id, created_at)` lets it skip the sort" |
| "It's more scalable" | "It removes the shared write path. The cost is eventual consistency in the read model" |

## Debugging questions want a process

When you hear "the API is returning 500s for some users," the interviewer is not hiding a specific bug. They made the scenario up. They want to hear what you would check first, what you would rule out, and what evidence would change your mind. Name the tool or signal at each step: "I would check whether it lines up with a deploy, then look at error rates by endpoint, then see whether the failures share a tenant or a payload shape."

Jumping straight to a guess scores worse than narrowing it down step by step, even when the guess happens to be right.

## Drills for one week

- **Pick the three tools you would call strengths.** For each, write the five families as questions and answer them out loud. Where you stall is what you need to read up on.
- **Explain one mechanism a day at two levels:** once to a junior engineer, once to the person who maintains the library.
- **Run an incident retro from memory.** What broke, what the first misleading signal was, and what actually fixed it.
- **Trim your resume.** If you cannot answer three of the families for something, consider taking it off. Interviewers choose topics from what you claim, which is also why the [ATS resume checklist](/blog/software-engineer-resume-ats-checklist) says to list only what you can defend.

## FAQ

### Is this the same as a system design round?

No. [System design](/blog/system-design-interview-prep-2026) asks you to design something new. This round questions what you have already built and the tools you built it with.

### What if I do not know the answer?

Say so in one sentence, then say how you would find out: "I do not remember the default isolation level there. I would check the docs and confirm with a quick concurrent test." That scores far better than inventing a mechanism.

### Do I need to rehearse out loud?

Yes. The round is entirely spoken, so written-only prep hurts even more here than in coding rounds; see [thinking out loud](/blog/coding-interview-communication).
