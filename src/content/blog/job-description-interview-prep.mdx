**Short answer:** for **job description interview prep**, treat the posting as a rough spec of the loop. The verbs ("design", "own", "mentor") tell you which rounds to expect. The technologies and domain words tell you what gets asked inside those rounds. Confirm the round list with the recruiter, then spend your prep time in proportion to those rounds.

Generic prep is how strong engineers end up studying for an interview they are not going to get. The hiring manager who wrote the posting often sits on the panel, and the text gives away more than most candidates notice.

## What a JD actually tells you

| Phrase in the JD | What it usually means for the loop |
|---|---|
| "Design and build systems at scale" | A design round with real depth, not a warm-up |
| "Own services end to end" | Operational questions: on-call, rollouts, failure modes |
| "Strong CS fundamentals" | A genuine algorithms round, not a formality |
| "Partner closely with product" | A stakeholder or prioritisation conversation |
| A named stack, repeated | A [stack-depth Q&A round](/blog/technical-qa-interview-round-prep) on exactly those tools |
| "Mentor and grow engineers" | Behavioural questions about feedback and scope |
| Domain words: payments, latency, ML | Domain questions where hand-waving shows |

Two passes are enough. On the first, highlight every **verb**: design, own, migrate, mentor, debug. Verbs map to rounds. On the second, highlight every **noun that is a technology or a domain**. Those map to the questions inside the rounds.

## Read the seniority bar, not the title

Titles vary wildly between companies. Responsibilities are more honest. "Contributes to" means you will be asked to execute. "Sets direction for" means you will be asked to defend a direction you chose and live with what followed. If the posting mentions ambiguity or cross-team influence, expect at least one question where the right answer is a trade-off rather than a solution. That is the same reasoning the [FAANG scoring rubrics](/blog/faang-interview-scoring) reward.

## Verify before you commit two weeks

Your reading of the JD is a guess. Cheap ways to check it:

- **Ask the recruiter for the loop structure.** Most will tell you the round types and lengths if you ask directly. This one email does more for your prep than anything else on this list.
- **Read the engineering blog.** It shows the real stack and the problems the team is proud of solving.
- **Look at the backgrounds of recent hires on the team.** It is a rough signal of who they bring in, not a rule.
- **Read recent candidate reports**, and discount anything more than a year old. Loops change, and [company differences are smaller than the folklore](/blog/faang-coding-interview-differences).

TechInView's setup flow can also generate a targeted loop from a company, role and pasted JD, which is a quick way to get a first draft of the round list. Treat it the same way: a draft you confirm with the recruiter.

## A two-week plan skeleton

Once you know the loop, allocate hours by round weight, not by what feels comfortable:

- **Days 1 to 2.** Write the rounds down and rate your readiness for each out of five. The lowest two get the most hours.
- **Days 3 to 7.** Depth on the weakest round. If it is coding, pick patterns that match the domain: payments work leans on precision and idempotency, ML infra leans on data volume and streaming. The [practice problem set](/practice) is enough to drill the core patterns.
- **Days 8 to 11.** Rehearse out loud. One timed mock per round type. Re-reading notes is not rehearsal.
- **Days 12 to 13.** Stories and specifics. Two per behavioural theme, with numbers you can quote. The [STAR guide](/blog/behavioral-interview-software-engineer-star) covers the structure.
- **Day 14.** Light review, sleep, and a short list of your own questions for them.

What is missing on purpose: a hundred extra problems in a category the JD never mentions. Broad coverage feels safe and costs a lot of hours. If the loop has one algorithms round and three conversational ones, your time should roughly follow that ratio. The [90-day plan](/blog/90-day-coding-interview-prep-plan) applies the same rule over a longer window.

## When the JD tells you nothing

Some postings are boilerplate copied across a dozen openings. When the text is empty, fall back on the company's shape. Early-stage startups tend to test breadth and how fast you ship. Large product companies usually split algorithms and design into separate rounds. Infrastructure teams push on failure modes. Then email the recruiter, because now you have a specific question instead of "what should I expect?"

## FAQ

### Should I mirror the JD's keywords in my answers?

Use its vocabulary where it is genuinely yours. Do not adopt terms you cannot defend, because the [stack-depth round](/blog/technical-qa-interview-round-prep) will find them.

### What if the JD asks for a technology I have never used?

Apply anyway if the fundamentals overlap. In the round, be direct: say what you have used that is similar, and what you would need a week to learn.

### How early should I do this?

Before the first call. The recruiter screen is the cheapest moment to ask about the loop.
